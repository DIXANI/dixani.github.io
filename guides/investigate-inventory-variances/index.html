How to Investigate Inventory Variances

A practical warehouse checklist for finding the cause of stock discrepancies before approving an inventory adjustment.

Published: September 6, 2026Updated: September 23, 2026By: Dinesh Madushanka

How do you investigate an inventory variance?

Compare the same item, unit, location, stock status and count cutoff. Recount independently, check other locations, trace receipts and issues, and separate confirmed findings from possible causes. Correct the appropriate transaction or obtain approval for an adjustment, then verify the final balance.

Follow the investigation checklist · See the worked example · Record the evidence

What is an inventory variance?

An inventory variance is the difference between the quantity recorded in the inventory system and the quantity physically counted within the same inventory scope. A negative variance means the physical quantity is lower than the system quantity. A positive variance means more stock was counted than the system shows.

BASIC FORMULA

Inventory variance = Physical quantity − System quantity

A variance should not be treated as an automatic instruction to adjust the system. It is a signal that a transaction, movement, count or control may need investigation. Correcting the number without identifying the cause can allow the same issue to happen again.

Inventory variance investigation checklist

Set the count scope and cutoff

Record the SKU, location, batch or serial where relevant, unit of measure and stock status. Save the system balance at the count time. Pause movements where practical; otherwise log receipts, picks and transfers during the count and reconcile them to that cutoff. Compare on-hand stock with on-hand stock, not with an available balance reduced by reservations.

Recount the stock independently

Ask a second person to recount the item without seeing the first count. Check unopened cartons, loose units and different units of measure. Confirm that carton quantity and individual-unit quantity were not confused.

Confirm the correct SKU and description

Compare the item code, barcode, description, pack size, batch and variant. Similar products, old item codes and nearly identical packaging frequently cause counting errors.

Check every possible storage location

Review the main bin, overflow racks, staging areas, receiving zones, dispatch lanes, quarantine, returns, damage areas and temporary locations. Stock may be physically present but stored outside its recorded bin.

Review recent receiving transactions

Check goods received notes, purchase orders and system receipts. Look for quantities received physically but not posted, duplicate receipts, incorrect pack conversions, rejected quantities or stock placed in another location.

Review picking and dispatch activity

Compare pick lists, goods issue notes, delivery documents and system issues. Check whether stock was picked but not confirmed, confirmed twice, short-picked, substituted or moved to dispatch staging.

Check transfers, returns and adjustments

Review inter-bin and inter-warehouse transfers, customer returns, supplier returns and recent adjustments. Confirm that both sides of each movement were completed and posted to the correct item and location.

Inspect damages, expiry and write-offs

Look for damaged, expired, obsolete, sampled or consumed stock that was physically removed but not recorded. Confirm that supporting forms and approvals match the system transaction.

Document the cause before adjustment

Record the confirmed quantities, variance, value, checks performed and supporting references. Label each proposed cause as confirmed, ruled out or unresolved. Assign an owner for remaining checks and obtain the required approval before any posting.

Common causes of stock discrepancies

  • Receiving errors: incorrect quantity, wrong SKU, duplicate receipt or unposted receipt.
  • Picking errors: wrong item, excess issue, short pick or unconfirmed dispatch.
  • Location errors: stock moved physically without a corresponding system transfer.
  • Unit-of-measure errors: cartons recorded as pieces, or pack-size conversions applied incorrectly.
  • Damage and expiry: unusable stock whose physical location or system status was not updated. Stock in quarantine may still be part of total on-hand stock.
  • Count errors: mixed batches, hidden stock, double counting or missed locations.
  • Master-data problems: duplicate SKUs, incorrect barcodes or outdated pack configurations.
  • Unauthorized movement: stock removed, used or relocated without documentation.

Worked example: investigate a 35-unit shortage

This fictional example uses one SKU, one unit of measure and the same stock scope at the count cutoff.

System stock: 1,250 units
Physical stock: 1,215 units

Variance: 1,215 − 1,250 = −35 units

If the unit cost is USD 8, the variance value is −35 × USD 8 = −USD 280.

The warehouse should not immediately reduce the system by 35 units. The investigation should first check recent receipts, dispatches, pending transfers, damage records, staging areas and units of measure. If the shortage remains unexplained after those checks, it should be documented and escalated according to the organization’s approval process.

What if an unposted dispatch explains 20 units?

  1. Verify the dispatch. Suppose the investigation confirms that 20 units left before the cutoff, belong to this SKU and scope, and have not already been issued in the system. A pick list alone is not proof that goods left; reconcile the shipment evidence and transaction history.
  2. Correct the source transaction. Follow the authorized issue-posting procedure. With no other movements in this example, the corrected system quantity is 1,250 − 20 = 1,230 units.
  3. Recalculate the remaining difference. Physical stock of 1,215 against corrected system stock of 1,230 leaves −15 units, worth −USD 120 at USD 8 each.
  4. Keep the remainder unresolved. Continue checking locations, transfers, receiving and stock status. Escalate the remaining difference with evidence if no cause is confirmed.

Do not post both the 20-unit issue and the original 35-unit shortage adjustment. That would correct the same 20 units twice. If subsequent movements occur, reconcile those movements before comparing the current system balance with the earlier count.

What should an investigation record include?

  • Case details: reference, SKU, UOM, warehouse/bin, batch or serial, stock status and count date/time.
  • Count evidence: original count, independent recount, counters and the system balance at the cutoff. Use a stock count sheet.
  • Transaction evidence: receipt, issue, transfer and return references, quantities, timestamps and posting status.
  • Findings: what each check established, conflicting evidence and any remaining unknowns. A stock difference alone does not prove theft or supplier error.
  • Decision and follow-up: proposed correction, reviewer/approver, action owner, due date, posting reference and final reconciliation.

When should inventory be adjusted?

An adjustment should be made only after the recount and transaction review are complete, the variance is documented, and the required approval has been obtained. The supporting record should show who counted the stock, who investigated it, the reason code used and who approved the correction.

  • Wrong location: verify both bins and use the appropriate transfer. A bin shortage and matching excess elsewhere may leave warehouse-level stock unchanged.
  • Missing or incorrect transaction: correct the receipt, issue, return or transfer through the authorized process where possible.
  • Wrong UOM: confirm the actual pack conversion and correct the affected records. Do not compare cartons directly with individual units.
  • Damaged or held stock: distinguish physical quantity from saleable status. A status change or approved write-off may be needed; do not automatically treat held stock as missing.
  • Confirmed residual difference: document the remaining variance and follow the required approval process for a quantity adjustment.

For damaged or expired stock, use the Damage / Write-off Form to record the affected quantities, evidence and required authorization. Confirm whether the correct action is a status change, supplier return or approved write-off before posting.

High-value, repeated or unusual variances should receive additional review. A pattern affecting the same SKU, shift, customer, supplier or location may indicate a process weakness rather than an isolated counting mistake.

Before closing the case, verify the posted transaction, reconcile the corrected balance and record who will check whether the corrective action prevents recurrence. Use thresholds and approval roles from your own procedure; this guide does not set a universal value limit.

Use the Inventory Adjustment Form to document a proposed correction and its approval. Attach the count and investigation evidence; completing a form does not authorize posting an adjustment.

How to reduce future variances

  • Use barcode scanning where practical and validate pack sizes in the item master.
  • Complete system transactions at the time of the physical movement.
  • Keep receiving, storage, quarantine and dispatch areas clearly separated.
  • Perform regular cycle counts based on item value, movement and risk.
  • Investigate recurring reason codes and assign corrective actions.
  • Train staff on location control, batch handling and units of measure.
  • Track variance quantity, variance value, count accuracy and repeat discrepancies.

Frequently asked questions

What if the inventory variance remains unexplained?

Record the checks completed and unresolved questions. Assign an investigation owner and escalate according to your organization's thresholds. Any authorized correction should retain an honest unresolved reason rather than inventing a cause.

Does damaged stock always mean a shortage?

No. Damaged stock can still be physically present and included in on-hand inventory. Compare matching stock statuses and determine whether a status change, disposal or approved write-off is required.

What should be checked first when stock does not match?

Start with an independent recount and confirm the SKU, unit of measure and storage location. Then review recent receipts, issues, transfers, returns and damage records.

Should the system be adjusted immediately after finding a variance?

No. Investigate and document the discrepancy first. The adjustment should follow the organization’s authorization procedure and include a clear reason.

What is a negative inventory variance?

A negative variance occurs when physical stock is lower than system stock. For example, 95 physical units against 100 system units produces a variance of −5 units.

How often should cycle counts be performed?

The frequency should reflect value, movement and risk. High-value or fast-moving items are normally counted more frequently than low-risk, slow-moving items.
