Warehouse Capacity and Storage Cost: Usable Pallet Positions and Cost per Position

Floor area is not storage capacity. Learn how to convert a building into usable pallet positions, what reduces that figure in practice, and how to calculate a defensible storage cost per position.

Published: October 20, 2026 Updated: October 20, 2026 By: Dinesh Madushanka

Floor area is not storage capacity

The most common warehouse capacity error is treating gross floor area as available storage. A building of 10,000 square metres does not offer 10,000 square metres of storage. A substantial share is consumed by aisles, staging, packing, dispatch, offices, welfare facilities, charging areas and columns before a single pallet is placed.

Capacity also comes in two different meanings that are often confused:

Static capacity

The total number of pallet positions that physically exist in the racking and floor areas, whether or not they are occupied. This is a property of the building and equipment.

Usable capacity

The number of positions that can realistically be filled given access, aisle widths, product mix, rack height limits and the need to keep operations moving. This is always lower than static capacity.

Productive capacity

The positions that can be turned over in a period given labour and equipment. A position holding slow-moving stock still consumes space even though it contributes little throughput.

Most planning failures come from quoting static capacity as if it were usable capacity. A rack may hold 5,000 pallets in theory, but if 30% of those positions are blocked, awkward, or unsuitable for the product being stored, the number that matters is closer to 3,500.

Calculating usable pallet positions

The calculation starts from clear storage area and works upward through racking levels. The sequence matters because each step removes space that cannot be recovered later.

USABLE PALLET POSITIONS

Positions ≈ (Clear storage area ÷ Position footprint) × Racking levels × Usable factor

Where the clear storage area is the floor area remaining after aisles, support areas and columns have been deducted, and the position footprint is the floor area one pallet position occupies including the share of uprights.

Step 1: Deduct non-storage space

Remove aisles, staging, packing, dispatch, offices, welfare, battery charging and any unbuildable zones around columns and sprinkler clearance.

Step 2: Divide by position footprint

A single pallet position on the floor occupies more than the pallet footprint. Include the access space required to place and retrieve it.

Step 3: Multiply by levels

Racking levels multiply the floor positions, but only up to the safe working height of the building and the reach of your handling equipment.

Step 4: Apply a usable factor

Reduce the theoretical figure for blocked locations, product incompatibilities, damaged racking and positions that are awkward to service.

Height, racking and the limits that cap capacity

Adding racking levels looks like the cheapest way to increase capacity, and it often is. But several independent limits cap how many levels are genuinely usable. Exceeding any one of them produces nominal capacity that cannot be used.

Building height and sprinkler clearance

Usable racking height is the building height minus the clearances required by fire protection and services. That headroom is not negotiable.

Equipment reach height

Racking above the safe working height of your forklifts or reach trucks cannot be serviced, regardless of how much building height exists above it.

Floor loading limits

High-bay racking concentrates load on small areas. The structural floor loading limit may cap levels before height does.

Aisle width versus equipment

Narrower aisles fit more positions on paper but may require different equipment. A narrow-aisle layout that slows every put-away can reduce effective throughput even as it increases position count.

Racking safety and inspection

Damaged uprights and beams reduce usable positions. Capacity should be based on the racking that is actually serviceable, not the total installed.

Calculating storage cost per pallet position

Once you know the number of usable positions, storage cost becomes a simple division. The difficulty is deciding which costs belong in the numerator. The defensible approach is to include the costs that exist because the warehouse exists, and exclude costs that vary directly with the volume of units handled.

STORAGE COST PER POSITION

Cost per position = (Total storage-related cost for the period) ÷ (Usable positions × Periods)

For an annual cost per position, divide the yearly storage cost by the number of usable positions. For a weekly figure, divide the annual result by 52.

The distinction matters because the purpose of the figure determines what belongs in it. A cost per position used to price storage services to a customer should include the full cost of providing the service. A cost per position used to compare two internal layouts should include only the costs that actually differ between them.

Worked example

Worked example

A warehouse has 8,000 square metres of gross area. Aisles and operational areas take 40%, leaving 4,800 square metres of clear storage area. Each pallet position, including upright share, occupies 1.2 square metres of floor, giving 4,000 floor positions.

With four racking levels that is 16,000 theoretical positions. Applying a 0.85 usable factor gives 13,600 usable positions.

Floor positions

4,800 ÷ 1.2 = 4,000 positions

With four racking levels

4,000 × 4 = 16,000 theoretical positions

Applying a 0.85 usable factor

16,000 × 0.85 = 13,600 usable positions

If total annual storage-related cost is 1,360,000, the cost per usable position is 1,360,000 ÷ 13,600 = 100 per position per year, or about 1.92 per week.

Now consider the effect of a common real-world problem. If 20% of positions are occupied by slow-moving stock that has not turned over in a year, those 2,720 positions are consuming 272,000 of storage cost annually while generating little throughput. Knowing the cost per position is what makes that waste visible and comparable.

COST OF LOW OCCUPANCY

Idle positions also carry cost. Space that is paid for but not filled is the most expensive storage of all.

Linking cost to utilization

Cost per position answers what a position costs. It does not answer whether you are using the positions well. Those are separate questions, and a warehouse can be expensive per position and still be poorly utilized.

Utilization is the ratio of occupied positions to usable positions. Low utilization means you are paying for space that produces nothing. Very high utilization is not automatically good either, because near-full occupancy creates congestion, slows put-away and picking, and leaves no room to absorb variability.

Combining the two figures is what makes the analysis actionable:

High cost, low utilization

The most expensive combination. You are paying for capacity that is not producing throughput. Review layout, product mix and whether the space is oversized for need.

High cost, high utilization

Expensive but productive. Focus shifts to reducing cost per position through layout density or rent renegotiation rather than filling more space.

Low cost, high utilization

Efficient operation, but little headroom. Watch for congestion and plan capacity before service levels degrade.

Low cost, low utilization

Cheap space used poorly. The opportunity is usually to consolidate stock and release space rather than to fill it at any cost.

This is why the companion guide on warehouse space utilization matters alongside cost. Cost tells you what the space is worth; utilization tells you whether you are getting it.

Common mistakes

Using gross area as storage area

Aisles, staging and support areas are not storage. Deducting them properly is the single biggest correction in most estimates.

Counting theoretical rather than usable positions

Blocked, damaged and inaccessible positions cannot be used. Quoting installed capacity overstates what the operation can hold.

Excluding non-rent costs

Racking maintenance, insurance, utilities and supervision are real storage costs. Omitting them understates the true figure.

Comparing cost per position across different definitions

Two figures built on different inclusions are not comparable. State what is included before drawing conclusions.

Ignoring the cost of idle space

Space paid for but unused still appears in the cost base. Utilization belongs in the same conversation as cost.

Adding racking levels without checking limits

Height, equipment reach and floor loading can each cap usable levels independently of building height.

Questions and answers

What share of warehouse area is typically usable for storage?

It varies widely with layout and operation, but clear storage area is often around 50% to 70% of gross area once aisles, staging, packing and support areas are deducted. Operations with heavy inbound or dispatch activity sit at the lower end.

Should storage cost per position include the cost of the inventory?

No. The value of the goods is a working capital cost, not a storage cost. Including it mixes two different decisions and makes the figure difficult to compare between products of different value.

How do I compare the cost of two warehouse layouts?

Include only the costs that differ between the two options. Rent may be identical in both, in which case it does not help distinguish them. Differences in racking, labour time and equipment requirements usually carry the decision.

Is higher pallet position utilization always better?

No. Near-full occupancy reduces the flexibility to absorb variability, increases congestion and slows access. Many operations find a healthy target range below maximum is more productive overall than full occupancy.

How often should capacity and cost per position be recalculated?

Review annually for cost, and whenever the layout, racking, product mix or contract changes materially. A cost per position based on a superseded layout will mislead pricing and investment decisions.
