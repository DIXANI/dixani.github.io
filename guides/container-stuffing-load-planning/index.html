Container Stuffing and Load Planning: Weight, Volume and Practical Limits

A container load is limited by the tightest of several constraints, not by volume alone. Learn how to reconcile payload, door opening, stacking strength and weight distribution before the load is built.

Published: October 20, 2026 Updated: October 20, 2026 By: Dinesh Madushanka

The constraints that actually limit a load

Container loading is often planned by cubic capacity alone, and that is the most common reason a load fails at the loading bay. Volume is only one of several independent limits, and the usable load is set by whichever binds first.

Internal volume

The cubic capacity inside the container. It sets a theoretical maximum that can never be fully achieved because cartons do not tile the space perfectly.

Payload limit

The maximum cargo weight, which is the container's maximum gross weight minus the tare weight of the container itself. Heavy products hit this long before volume is exhausted.

Door opening

Cargo must pass through the door aperture, which is smaller than the internal cross-section. A load that fits inside may not fit through the doorway.

Stacking strength

Cartons have a compression limit. Stack height is capped by what the bottom cartons can bear, not by the height of the container.

Weight distribution

Load must be balanced and secured. Concentrating mass at one end or one side creates handling and safety problems.

PAYLOAD LIMIT

Available payload = Maximum gross weight − Container tare weight

Because these constraints bind independently, the practical load is the minimum of what volume allows, what payload allows and what packaging and door dimensions allow. A plan built on volume alone will overstate the load whenever weight, door size or carton strength becomes the tighter limit.

Volume, and why the theoretical maximum is never reached

Cubic capacity is calculated from internal dimensions, but the usable volume is always lower. Cartons are rectangular, containers have corrugations and door frames, and pallets do not fill every remaining gap.

Practical volume utilization is commonly in the region of 70% to 85% for well-planned carton loads, and lower when cartons are of mixed sizes, are irregularly shaped, or are palletized with overhanging loads. Two effects drive the loss:

Tiling loss

Cartons arranged in a grid leave leftover strips along the container walls when the carton dimension does not divide evenly into the internal dimension.

Stacking and dunnage loss

Slip sheets, airbags, securing straps and load restraint all consume space that cannot then be used for cargo.

Weight limits and the heavy-cargo case

For dense products, payload is reached well before the container looks full. This is normal and not a planning failure, but it must be anticipated because the consequences are expensive.

Know the tare weight

Payload is not the same as maximum gross weight. Using gross weight as if it were available cargo weight overstates capacity by the weight of the container itself.

Check road limits, not just the container

A container can be legal at sea and still overload a road vehicle once chassis and tractor weights are included. Road limits often bind first on inland legs.

Confirm weighbridge practice

Verified gross mass requirements mean the declared weight must be accurate. An estimate that ignores packaging and pallets can leave a container unable to sail.

Watch the weight of packaging

Pallets, crates, strapping and dunnage add weight that is easy to overlook. For dense cargo this can be a meaningful share of payload.

Door openings and access

The door aperture is smaller than the container's internal cross-section, and this catches out loads that were planned purely from internal dimensions. Cargo that fits inside the container but cannot pass through the door must be repacked, which is rarely cheap.

Check the door height and width, not the internal ones

Use the actual door opening dimensions for anything being loaded as a single unit, such as crated machinery or tall palletized loads.

Allow for handling equipment clearance

A forklift mast, a pallet truck or a load on forks needs vertical and lateral clearance to pass the door frame safely.

Account for door hardware and seals

Locking bars and door frames intrude into the aperture. The clear opening is smaller than the nominal door size.

Plan the loading sequence

Items loaded first must not block access to items loaded later. Load sequence is part of the plan, not an afterthought at the bay.

Stacking strength and load stability

Carton compression strength decreases with time, humidity and stacking height. A load that looks stable at the moment of stuffing can deform in transit, shifting weight and potentially collapsing.

Respect the compression limit

Stack height should be set by what the bottom cartons can bear over the duration of the journey, including a safety margin for transit vibration.

Consider moisture and duration

Long sea voyages expose cartons to sustained humidity, which reduces strength. A stack that survives a short road trip may not survive weeks at sea.

Interlock rather than column stack where possible

Some interlocking improves stability, though not all packaging permits it without compromising the carton.

Secure the load, do not rely on friction alone

Dunnage, airbags, straps or blocking prevent movement that can shift weight and damage cargo or the container.

Weight distribution and stability

Even a load within the payload limit can be loaded badly. Weight distribution affects handling, vehicle stability and the structural load on the container floor.

Keep the centre of gravity low and central

Heavy items should sit low and toward the middle rather than high up or concentrated at one end.

Avoid concentrating mass at one end

Heavy cargo concentrated at the door end or the far end creates uneven loading that can stress the container and the carrying vehicle.

Balance side to side

Lateral imbalance affects stability during handling and road transport, particularly on curves and ramps.

Respect floor loading limits

Point loads from heavy, small-footprint items can exceed the container floor's rated capacity even when total weight is compliant.

Worked example

Suppose a 40-foot general purpose container has an internal volume of about 67 cubic metres and a maximum payload of about 26,500 kilograms. A shipment consists of cartons measuring 0.6 × 0.4 × 0.4 metres, weighing 12 kilograms each.

Volume limit

Each carton is 0.096 cubic metres. At 67 cubic metres of internal volume, a theoretical 697 cartons fit before allowing for tiling and dunnage losses.

Practical volume limit

At 80% practical utilization, around 558 cartons can be loaded by volume.

Weight check

558 cartons at 12 kilograms is 6,696 kilograms, comfortably below the payload limit.

Binding constraint

Volume binds, so the practical answer is the volume figure. Payload is not the limiting factor for this product.

Now change the product to a dense item weighing 60 kilograms per carton of the same size. The volume limit still suggests 558 cartons, but that would weigh 33,480 kilograms, exceeding the payload limit of 26,500 kilograms. Payload now binds, and the real maximum is 26,500 ÷ 60 = about 441 cartons, leaving roughly 20% of the volume empty.

THE PRACTICAL RULE

The load is limited by the tightest constraint. Calculate volume and payload separately, then take the lower figure.

Where payload is the binding constraint, adding volume is pointless. The planning question becomes how to use the weight allowance productively rather than how to fill the remaining air.

Common mistakes

Planning by cubic volume alone

Payload, door openings and stacking strength can each bind before volume does. A volume-only plan overstates the load for heavy or bulky products.

Confusing gross weight with payload

Payload is maximum gross weight minus tare weight. Treating gross weight as available cargo weight overstates capacity.

Ignoring the door opening

The aperture is smaller than the internal cross-section. Large single units can fail to enter even when they would fit inside.

Assuming cartons stack to the roof

Compression strength, not container height, usually caps the stack.

Forgetting packaging and dunnage weight

Pallets, crates and restraint systems add both weight and volume that reduce usable capacity.

Loading heavy items high or to one end

Poor weight distribution affects stability and can stress the container floor or the carrying vehicle.

Questions and answers

What is the difference between container capacity and payload?

Capacity usually refers to volume, the cubic space inside the container. Payload refers to weight, the maximum cargo mass allowed after deducting the container's tare weight from its maximum gross weight. A load can be within one limit and over the other.

Why can't I use the full internal volume?

Cartons do not tile the internal space perfectly, and dunnage, slip sheets and restraint systems consume space. Practical utilization is commonly around 70% to 85% for well-planned carton loads.

How do I know which limit applies to my shipment?

Calculate the volume-based carton count and the weight-based carton count separately. The lower of the two is the binding constraint. Dense products usually hit weight first; light, bulky products usually hit volume first.

Does the door opening really matter for cartons?

For loose cartons that pass through individually, rarely. For palletized loads, crated equipment or anything entering as a single unit, the door aperture is often the tightest constraint and must be checked explicitly.

How much weight can be stacked on a carton?

That depends on the packaging specification and its compression strength, which decreases with time and humidity. The manufacturer's rating should be treated as a maximum and reduced for long sea voyages.

Should I use a pallet or load loose cartons?

Pallets speed handling and reduce damage but consume both volume and weight. Loading loose cartons maximizes density. The choice depends on labour costs, handling equipment at both ends and product fragility rather than on container capacity alone.
